Before you ask

The questions your technical evaluator will raise.

Answered here so you don't need a call to get to the interesting part.

How is this different from a classifier?

A classifier is trained on a fixed label set. Verdict takes options[] as a request parameter, so the same model handles "approve/hold/decline" at 9am and "tier_1/tier_2/tier_3" at 9:05. No retraining, no redeploy.

What's the accuracy versus an LLM?

On structured decision tasks we match or exceed prompted GPT-class models on top-1 accuracy, with calibration error under 2%. Full confidence intervals and per-domain breakdowns are in the benchmark appendix.

Can it run inside our infrastructure?

Yes. Deploy in your VPC or fully self-hosted. The model is a single container with no external calls — no data leaves your network, no egress costs, no third-party subprocessor.

What's the failure mode?

Verdict can only return one of the options you supplied, in a fixed schema. It cannot invent a category or emit free text. When confidence falls below your threshold, it returns escalate: true instead of guessing.

Ledgerline, a payments processor handling 2.1 million monthly transactions, was losing good customers to a fraud queue that took four hours to clear. After a six-week evaluation, they replaced their rules engine and a prompted-LLM pilot with Verdict — cutting median review time to 90 seconds, reducing false positives by 62%, and recovering $1.4M in annual revenue that had been leaking to over-blocking.

“We weren't choosing a model. We were choosing whether our analysts would still have a job in two years — and whether our good customers would still be here.”

Dana Whitfield · VP Risk Operations, Ledgerline
01 — Situation

A queue that grew faster than the team

Ledgerline's risk team had 14 analysts working a queue of roughly 9,000 flagged transactions per day. Each review took a median of four hours end-to-end, and the backlog meant most cases were resolved long after the customer had already abandoned the purchase.

The cost wasn't just operational. Roughly one in five holds turned out to be a legitimate customer — and Ledgerline's own data showed that a customer who experiences a fraud hold is 3.4× more likely to churn within 90 days.

02 — Evaluation

Four options, three criteria that mattered

The team spent six weeks evaluating four approaches. They were explicit about what would make them walk away:

  • p99 latency under 200ms. Anything slower couldn't sit inline in the authorization path — it would have to stay async, which defeats the purpose.
  • No hallucinated output formats. The downstream system parses a strict schema. A single malformed response at 3am pages an on-call engineer.
  • Adding a new decline reason without retraining. Their fraud taxonomy changed roughly every six weeks. A model that needed a training cycle for each change was a non-starter.

A prompted LLM passed the flexibility test but failed on latency (p99 ~6.4s) and output reliability (71% parseable in their harness). Expanding the rules engine passed latency but failed the adaptability test — the rule tree was already 4,000 branches deep. A fine-tuned classifier passed latency and reliability but required a retraining cycle for every taxonomy change.

Verdict was the only option that cleared all three criteria in the pilot.

03 — Result

Ninety seconds, and a queue that clears itself

Ledgerline deployed Verdict inside their own VPC, inline in the authorization path. Low-confidence decisions still escalate to a human — but that's now roughly 8% of volume instead of 100%.
